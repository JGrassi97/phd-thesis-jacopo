\noindent\textit{The research presented in this chapter was the final component of the dissertation to be undertaken and, unlike the work described in the subsequent chapters, has not yet been the subject of a dedicated journal publication. Its two components are currently at different stages of development. The software framework for running AI weather models on high-performance computing systems was developed during my visiting research period at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center and has already supported other research applications. My contribution focused on the design of the framework's architectural abstractions and the implementation of inference support for two widely used models: AIFS and NeuralGCM. The scientific validation framework is more recent and represents an initial methodological contribution, with further development and testing still ongoing. The chapter is nevertheless presented first because it addresses the production of climate data and thus provides the logical starting point for the progression developed throughout the dissertation.}

In 1974, \citet{schneider_climate_1974} described the development of climate models as a means ``first to derive better understanding and ultimately to allow some degree of predictive capability.'' Their review connected this scientific ambition to pressing societal concerns, including climate-related food shortages and the possibility that human activities could alter the climate. The prospect was that a quantitative representation of the climate system could eventually help societies anticipate changes whose consequences extended beyond atmospheric science.

Over the following decades, advances in physical understanding, numerical methods, observations, and computing transformed this prospect into an established scientific capability. Climate models developed into coupled representations of the atmosphere, ocean, land surface, and cryosphere, with Earth System Models incorporating additional biogeochemical processes. Coordinated experiments now provide projections of climate change under different forcing scenarios \citep{eyring_overview_2016}. Combined with models of exposed natural and human systems, these simulations support assessments of climate impacts across sectors and geographical scales \citep{huber_climate_2014}. Climate modelling has therefore become a foundation for investigating future risks and producing the data on which many Climate Services depend.

Over the last few years, Artificial Intelligence has introduced new approaches to simulating weather and climate. Models trained on atmospheric reanalyses can now generate global weather forecasts with skill comparable to, and for many evaluated variables exceeding, that of established numerical forecasting systems. These developments encompass a range of architectures and training strategies, from fully data-driven forecasting systems to the integration of learned components into physical models \citep{de_burgh-day_machine_2023}. In parallel, climate emulators have been developed to reproduce atmospheric evolution and climate statistics at substantially lower computational cost. For example, ACE2 can generate stable multi-decadal atmospheric simulations and reproduce historical variability and temperature trends under prescribed boundary conditions and forcings \citep{watt-meyer_ace2_2025}. These developments open opportunities for producing larger ensembles and exploring atmospheric variability more extensively. 

Despite their rapid progress, the suitability of AI weather and climate models for Climate Services cannot be established from aggregate forecast scores alone. Evaluation must also consider the representation of extremes, consistency between variables, and climatic variability \citep{schultz_can_2021}. In deterministic forecasting, minimizing mean squared error can favour increasingly smooth predictions as uncertainty grows \citep{lang_aifs-crps_2026}. Moreover, \citet{sun_can_2025} showed that versions of FourCastNet with similar global verification scores differed substantially in their ability to predict intense tropical cyclones absent from their training data.

The development of these models also depends on the quality and availability of their training data. Many leading systems rely on reanalyses such as ERA5, which combine observations and numerical forecasts through data assimilation \citep{hersbach_era5_2020}. Continued investment in observations, physical modelling, and reanalysis production therefore remains important, alongside emerging approaches trained directly on observations \citep{mcnally_data_2024}. This dependence also creates a risk of inheriting systematic errors: global coverage does not imply uniform data quality, and agreement with a training reference does not guarantee equivalent agreement with independent observations. As argued by \citet{mozaffari_rise_2026}, uneven data quality and unequal access to computational infrastructure can also concentrate the benefits of AI in the most developed regions. 

These considerations motivate a more systematic approach to executing and evaluating AI weather models for climate applications. The evaluations accompanying new model releases provide essential evidence of their capabilities, but their conclusions remain conditional on the selected datasets, initialization procedures, forecast horizons, and verification criteria. Establishing their suitability for Climate Services requires extending these assessments to additional experimental configurations, including initialization from climate simulations and the evaluation of climatic statistics beyond conventional forecast skill.

Shared inference tools, including ECMWF's \emph{ai-models}\footnote{An open-source Python package providing a common command-line interface for running AI weather models through model-specific plugins, with support for retrieving initial conditions and managing forecast outputs. See \url{https://github.com/ecmwf-lab/ai-models}.} and NVIDIA's Earth2Studio\footnote{An open-source Python framework offering a unified interface and modular components for constructing AI weather and climate inference workflows, including data sources, pretrained models, ensemble generation, and output handling. See \url{https://nvidia.github.io/earth2studio/}.}, have reduced the technical barriers to running different models, while WeatherBench~2 provides a common framework for forecast verification \citep{rasp_weatherbench_2024}. Building on these developments, a further requirement is to connect data preparation, ensemble execution, output harmonization, and scientific evaluation within reproducible experiments. This becomes particularly relevant when comparing models initialized from heterogeneous datasets, managing many initialization dates and ensemble members, and examining how their behaviour changes with forecast lead time.

One potential application of AI weather models is to augment existing numerical climate simulations with additional realizations of weather evolution at a substantially lower computational cost. Repeatedly initializing stochastic AI forecasts from atmospheric states sampled along a climate simulation could improve the sampling of weather variability conditional on that simulated climate. This use of AI forecasts as a complement to conventional climate simulations has been proposed by \citet{rackow_robustness_2024}, while large AI-generated weather ensembles have demonstrated the benefits of increased sampling for characterizing variability and extremes \citep{mahesh_huge_2025}. Such an application requires both a workflow capable of initializing and coordinating the forecasts and evidence that the additional realizations remain statistically consistent with the source climate.

This chapter connects these two requirements through the development and application of AIUQ. During my research visit to the Barcelona Supercomputing Center, I brought the workflow's development to completion, building on an initial Autosubmit prototype developed by the host team. I introduced an abstraction layer separating initial condition preparation from model-specific inference and implemented support for AIFS-CRPS. As an application of this infrastructure, I conceived a comparative experiment using NeuralGCM and AIFS-CRPS initialized from EERIE-AMIP simulations. The experiment evaluates forecast skill, climatological agreement, stability, and anomaly amplitude, providing an initial assessment of whether these models can support the proposed augmentation of climate simulations.

\section{Methods}

The methods connect the practical requirements of generating AI ensembles from climate simulations with the scientific assessment of their suitability for this purpose. The intended application is to expand the sampling of weather variability around states drawn from a physics-based climate simulation at a reduced computational cost. AIUQ provides the infrastructure for preparing initial conditions, coordinating ensemble forecasts, and harmonizing outputs across models. Using this workflow, the experiment examines whether NeuralGCM and AIFS-CRPS initialized from EERIE-AMIP produce additional weather realizations that remain consistent with the source climate. The following subsections describe the workflow, the experimental configuration, and the evaluation criteria used to assess this prerequisite.

\subsection{AIUQ Workflow} \label{workflow}

AIUQ is organized as a modular workflow in which orchestration, data preparation, model execution, and output harmonization are separate concerns. The workflow is coordinated by Autosubmit \citep{manubens-gil_seamless_2016}, while the scientific transformations are implemented by source- and model-specific adapters. At the centre of the architecture is the AIUQ standard, which acts as a data contract between initial-condition (IC) sources and forecasting models. Consequently, an IC source only needs to produce the standard representation and a model only needs to consume it; no dedicated interface is required for every source--model pair. Figure~\ref{fig:aiuq-pipeline} summarizes the resulting pipeline.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{Chapter2/Figs/aiuq-pipeline.png}
    \caption{Overview of the AIUQ workflow. Source-specific adapters retrieve or read ICs and normalize them to the AIUQ standard. Model-specific adapters translate this common representation to the requirements of the selected checkpoint, while the post-processing stage harmonizes the requested forecast outputs.}
    \label{fig:aiuq-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Configuration} \label{configuration}

An AIUQ experiment is defined declaratively through YAML configuration files. The user selects the forecasting model and checkpoint, the IC source, the initialization dates, the ensemble members, and the temporal chunking of the simulation. The same configuration also describes the requested output variables, pressure levels, temporal frequency, and horizontal resolution. Platform-specific options specify where each task is executed and which computational resources it requires, together with the paths to auxiliary files needed for the execution. At submission time, Autosubmit constructs the experiment graph, combining the specific jobs for the retrieval and preparation of the ICs and for running the inference with the AI models.  

\paragraph{ICs preparation} \label{ics-preparation}

The ICs preparation stage converts heterogeneous input data into a source independent representation. A source adapter either retrieves the requested fields from an online archive or reads them from local storage. Each source is described by an IC card containing its variable names, identifiers, vertical coordinates, and access-specific metadata. These entries are matched against the selected version of the AIUQ standard, after which the retrieved dataset is subset, renamed, and assigned the common coordinates and attributes. Pressure levels and horizontal coordinates are harmonized when necessary. If the primary source does not provide all fields required by the standard, AIUQ invokes the configured fallback hierarchy. 

\paragraph{Fallbacks} \label{fallbacks}
An IC source may not provide every field defined by the AIUQ standard. During IC preparation, AIUQ compares the variable identifiers declared in the source card with those required by the selected standard version. To fill the gaps AIUQ applies a deterministic fallback hierarchy. Missing time-invariant surface fields are first obtained from a shared static dataset; these include orography, the land--sea mask, and descriptors of sub-grid-scale orography obtained from ECMWF. Fields that remain unavailable can then be supplied from a climatological dataset obtained from ERA5. 

\paragraph{Model inference} \label{model-inference}

At the inference stage, the model chosen in the configuration is fed with the ICs prepared at the stage before. A model card maps AIUQ standard to the specific input expected by the model. The adapter then performs only the transformations intrinsic to that backend, such as selecting fields, interpolating to the model grid, changing array layout, or constructing the model's native input state. Inference is executed inside a model-specific container using a versioned checkpoint stored outside the workflow repository. For stochastic models, the ensemble member identifier is also used to initialize the model, making individual realizations reproducible. Model states can be checkpointed where supported, so long integrations such as climate scale simulations can be divided into manageable segments and resumed after interruptions.

\paragraph{Post-processing} \label{post-processing}

The final stage transforms native forecasts into standardized output. AIUQ retains only the variables and vertical levels requested by the user, translates model specific names back to the common convention, and applies the selected temporal aggregation and horizontal regridding. This design avoids storing the full native output when only a subset is needed and makes the computational and storage cost of an experiment configurable. Processed fields are written as NetCDF files organized by variable, ensemble member, and forecast interval, with consistent temporal and spatial coordinates. The resulting simulations therefore share a predictable layout across models and IC sources and can be compared directly or passed to downstream components.


\subsection{Experiment configuration} \label{experiment-configuration}

This study uses ERA5 and the EERIE-AMIP simulations, both representing historical climate conditions. ERA5 \citep{hersbach_era5_2020} is a widely used global reanalysis that combines historical observations with a numerical weather prediction model through data assimilation, providing a reconstruction of past atmospheric conditions. The dataset currently covers the period from 1940 to the present \citep{soci_era5_2024}. ERA5 is extensively used to train AI weather models and serves as a common reference for evaluating their forecasts. Initializing and verifying these models against ERA5 therefore provides a reference configuration closely aligned with their training data in terms of data source. We regard this as an approximately in-distribution setting, without assuming that the training and evaluation distributions are identical. ERA5 fields were retrieved from the public Analysis-Ready, Cloud-Optimized (ARCO) archive hosted on Google Cloud Storage \citep{carver_arco-era5_2023}.

The EERIE-AMIP simulations \citep{aengenheyster_multi-decadal_2026} were produced at ECMWF using an atmosphere-only configuration of the Integrated Forecasting System (IFS), covering 1980--2023. Initialized from ERA5 in January 1980, the simulations evolve freely without atmospheric data assimilation, with prescribed sea surface temperatures, sea ice concentrations, and external radiative forcings. They therefore generate their own weather trajectories rather than reconstructing the observed sequence of weather events. Their climatology is expected to broadly resemble the observed historical climate, although systematic model biases remain. Although ERA5 and EERIE-AMIP both use the IFS, they differ in model configuration and observational constraints. Initializing and verifying AI forecasts against EERIE-AMIP thus provides a potentially out-of-distribution setting, testing generalization to model atmospheric states under broadly comparable historical climate conditions. EERIE-AMIP fields were retrieved through ECMWF's Meteorological Archival and Retrieval System (MARS).

I used two AI-based global weather forecasting models, NeuralGCM and AIFS-CRPS. NeuralGCM \citep{kochkov_neural_2024} combines a differentiable atmospheric dynamical core with neural networks that represent unresolved physical processes, such as cloud formation, radiation, and convection. The model is trained end-to-end on ERA5 trajectories and supports both deterministic and stochastic configurations. In the stochastic configuration, spatially and temporally correlated noise is incorporated into the learned parameterizations, enabling ensemble forecasting. In the original evaluation, the stochastic model at approximately $1.4^\circ$ resolution achieved lower ensemble-mean RMSE and CRPS than the operational ECMWF ensemble for most evaluated variables and lead times up to 15 days. Separate deterministic configurations also reproduced key climate statistics in multi-year to multi-decadal simulations with prescribed sea surface temperature and sea ice.

AIFS-CRPS \citep{lang_aifs-crps_2026} is a probabilistic forecasting model developed at ECMWF. Its encoder--processor--decoder architecture combines an attention mechanisms with a transformer processor and advances the atmospheric state in six-hour steps. The model is trained on ERA5, with operational IFS analyses used for additional training of the medium-range configurations. Ensemble members are generated through stochastic noise injected into the network, while an almost fair CRPS training objective jointly rewards forecast accuracy and appropriate ensemble spread. Published evaluations showed improved CRPS relative to the physics-based IFS ensemble for most variables and lead times up to 15 days. Experiments with a lower-resolution configuration also demonstrated competitive skill for weekly anomalies in 46-day forecasts, extending its potential applications to subseasonal prediction.

For both AI models, the experimental configuration comprises 10-member ensemble forecasts initialized every 10 days during 1985--1990 from EERIE-AMIP member~1, with each forecast extending to 30 days. The initialization schedule samples atmospheric conditions throughout the seasonal cycle and across multiple years. The 30-day integrations allow the evaluation to follow the transition from strong initialization dependence towards increasingly divergent weather trajectories, while detecting possible systematic drift or instability.

This design provides an initial test of prerequisites for augmenting climate simulations. In particular, additional ensemble members are useful for this purpose only if their spread represents plausible variability without introducing substantial distortions of the source climate. The full ten-member EERIE-AMIP ensemble and ERA5 provide complementary comparison datasets. All datasets are evaluated as daily means on a common $1^\circ \times 1^\circ$ latitude--longitude grid, focusing on temperature at 850~hPa.

\subsection{Evaluation criteria}
\label{evaluation}

The evaluation addresses two complementary aspects of model performance: the accuracy of ensemble forecasts in predicting the evolution of individual weather trajectories, and their ability to reproduce the climatological mean fields of the reference datasets. Forecast accuracy is assessed using the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS), while climatological agreement is evaluated through global mean bias and spatial root mean square error (RMSE). All metrics are calculated on the common verification grid. Spatial averages are weighted on the cosine of the latitude:

\begin{equation}
    w_i = \frac{A_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} A_j},
\end{equation}

where $A_i$ is the area of grid cell $i$ and $N$ is the number of grid cells.

\paragraph{Ensemble forecast accuracy}

The CRPS evaluates a predictive distribution against a verifying value \citep{gneiting_strictly_2007}. For a forecast cumulative distribution function $F$ and a reference value $r$, it is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CRPS}(F,r)
    =
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    \left[F(x)-H(x-r)\right]^2 \,\mathrm{d}x,
    \label{eq:crps}
\end{equation}
where $H(x-r)$ is the Heaviside step function, equal to zero for $x<r$ and one for $x\geq r$. For an ensemble of $M$ equally weighted members with predicted values $x_1,\ldots,x_M$, the CRPS of the empirical forecast distribution can equivalently be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CRPS}(F,r)
    =
    \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}|x_k-r|
    -
    \frac{1}{2M^2}
    \sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|x_k-x_j|.
    \label{eq:crps-ensemble}
\end{equation}
The score accounts for both the location and spread of the forecast distribution. Lower values indicate better performance, and the score has the same physical units as the evaluated variable. For a deterministic forecast, it reduces to the absolute error.

For the EERIE-initialized experiments, the verifying values are taken from the evolving EERIE-AMIP member~1 trajectory, which also supplies the initial conditions. This comparison therefore evaluates the ability of the AI models to predict the subsequent evolution of the source simulation. The CRPS is calculated locally before spatial averaging with the weights $w_i$, and the resulting global scores are averaged across initialization dates at each lead time.

\paragraph{Climatological agreement}

Daily climatologies are constructed for each day of the year over the study period, with forecast values assigned according to their valid date. These climatologies also retain information about forecast lead time, defined as the time elapsed since initialization. At short lead times, the predicted atmospheric states remain closely tied to the initial conditions and therefore to the source dataset. As lead time increases, this influence gradually weakens, and the forecasts increasingly reflect each AI model's own dynamics and systematic tendencies. Retaining the lead-time dimension therefore allows us to examine how the model climatology evolves during the forecast and whether it progressively departs from that of the initialization dataset.

Climatological differences are evaluated using the area-weighted global mean bias and spatial RMSE. The bias indicates whether the model overestimates or underestimates the reference global mean:

$$
\mathrm{Bias}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i
\left(\overline{X}^{A}_{i}-\overline{X}^{R}_{i}\right)
$$

The RMSE measures the magnitude of spatial differences, capturing regional errors that may cancel in the global mean:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE}
    =
    \sqrt{
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i
    \left(\overline{X}^{A}_{i}-\overline{X}^{R}_{i}\right)^2
    } 
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Anomaly amplitude and spatial structure}
Daily temperature anomalies are evaluated relative to a common EERIE-AMIP climatology for 1985--1990. Using the same reference for all datasets allows the analysis to identify departures from the source climate, including both systematic offsets and differences in anomaly amplitude. The diagnostics are calculated separately for each ensemble member, using a randomly selected subset of initialization dates and the corresponding EERIE-AMIP fields.

For initialization $s$, member $m$, grid cell $i$, and lead time $\tau$, the anomaly is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    A_{s,m,i}(\tau) = T_{s,m,i}(\tau) - C^{E}_{i}\bigl(d_s(\tau)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $C^{E}$ denotes the EERIE-AMIP climatology and $d_s(\tau)$ identifies the day of the year of the forecast valid date. The spatial RMS is:
\begin{equation}
    R_{s,m}(\tau) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i A_{s,m,i}(\tau)^2},
    \label{eq:anomaly-rms}
\end{equation}
with normalized cosine-latitude weights $w_i$. The anomaly-amplitude curves show these RMS values averaged across initializations, with an additional average across members for the ensemble summary.

To distinguish globally uniform departures from geographical structure, each anomaly field is decomposed into its global mean and a residual:
\begin{equation}
    G_{s,m}(\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i A_{s,m,i}(\tau),
    \qquad 
    B_{s,m,i}(\tau) = A_{s,m,i}(\tau)-G_{s,m}(\tau).
\end{equation}
The corresponding RMS components are calculated as
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{global}}(\tau) = \sqrt{\left\langle G_{s,m}(\tau)^2\right\rangle_{s,m}},
    \qquad
    R_{\mathrm{spatial}}(\tau) =
    \sqrt{ \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i B_{s,m,i}(\tau)^2 \right\rangle_{s,m}},
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle_{s,m}$ denotes averaging over
initializations and members. 

The geographical distribution of residual anomaly amplitude is examined through local RMS maps. At each grid cell, squared residual anomalies are averaged across members and days within the lead-time windows $[5,10)$, $[10,15)$, and $[20,25)$, and subsequently across initialization dates before taking the square root. Maps show the difference between the resulting AI and EERIE-AMIP RMS fields; positive values indicate larger residual anomaly amplitudes in the AI forecasts. The final window is omitted for AIFS-CRPS because of its deterioration at longer leads. These diagnostics retain regional systematic offsets and therefore describe departures from the common reference climatology, rather than unbiased temporal variability alone.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Computational performance and forecast stability}
\label{sec:performance-stability}

The initial assessment examines the computational feasibility of the experiment and the behaviour of the forecasts over the 30-day integration period. Selected forecasts initialized in 1987 illustrate the main features.

Figure~\ref{fig:example-inference-maps} compares the ensemble-mean temperature at 850~hPa from both AI models with the corresponding EERIE-AMIP member~1 field, ten days after initialization. Both models retain the broad geographical distribution of temperature, including the meridional gradient and the main continental thermal contrasts. Their ensemble means are smoother than the individual EERIE-AMIP realization, consistent with averaging members whose weather patterns progressively diverge. Substantial regional differences nevertheless emerge. The bias patterns of the two models are notably similar, with a spatial correlation coefficient of approximately $r=0.85$ in this example.

The global mean temperature provides a complementary view of forecast evolution (Figure~\ref{fig:global-temperature}). For the three initialization dates shown, AIFS-CRPS develops a recurring cooling tendency during the latter part of the integration, beginning around days 15--20. This behaviour is particularly pronounced in summer (Figure~\ref{fig:global-temperature-summer}), suggesting a forecast drift whose magnitude depends on season and lead time. NeuralGCM does not exhibit the same late-stage cooling in these examples, although its global mean temperature frequently exceeds the EERIE-AMIP reference.

Forecast accuracy was assessed using the CRPS, with the evolving EERIE-AMIP member~1 trajectory as the verification reference (Figure~\ref{fig:crps}). A supplementary NeuralGCM experiment initialized and verified against ERA5 provides a comparison using the same data source employed during training. CRPS generally increases with lead time and approaches a plateau after approximately 10--15 days. Both EERIE-initialized models exhibit scores of a similar magnitude to the ERA5 experiment, suggesting that initialization from EERIE-AMIP does not produce a markedly larger aggregate forecast error in the samples examined. However, the different verification datasets and initialization samples prevent this comparison from establishing equivalent forecast skill. The plateau describes the saturation of this aggregate error measure and does not demonstrate complete loss of initialization influence.

Visual inspection also reveals increasingly anomalous AIFS-CRPS structures over complex terrain. In the Himalayan example (Figure~\ref{fig:example-aifs-degenerative}), temperature and geopotential height patterns remain broadly comparable to EERIE-AMIP at day~15. By day~25, tightly packed, closed geopotential height contours develop around the Tibetan Plateau, intensifying further by day~29 alongside growing temperature differences. Similar artefacts beneath elevated terrain at long lead times are documented by ECMWF for AIFS ENS \citep{ecmwf_known_2026}. During AIFS-CRPS development, some experimental configurations also exhibited artificial growth of small-scale geopotential variability, which propagated towards larger scales \citep{lang_aifs-crps_2026}. This identifies a possible contributing mechanism, but does not establish the cause of the anomalies observed here. Moreover, the 850~hPa surface lies below the terrain across much of this region, where pressure-level fields depend on below-ground extrapolation \citep{ecmwf_atmospheric_2019}. The contribution of these regional artefacts and below-ground values to the global cooling remains unresolved. Their occurrence alongside the temperature drift motivates further investigation, but the present diagnostics do not establish a causal connection.

Together, the recurrent global cooling and growing regional anomalies limit the suitability of AIFS-CRPS for generating climate data over the full 30-day period. In the version~1.0 configuration examined here, deterioration becomes apparent after approximately 15--20 days. 

From a computational perspective, both models allowed the experiment to be completed within manageable times. For each model, generating one year of initialization dates, with starts every ten days, ten ensemble members per start, and 30-day forecasts, required approximately one hour on MareNostrum5 using ten GPUs in parallel. The corresponding workload required approximately one day on FELIPE, a computing system at Politecnico di Torino equipped with two GPUs. These timings describe the complete set of forecasts initialized within one year, rather than a continuous year-long integration.

\subsection{AI Model Climatology}

The climatological evaluation examines whether forecasts initialized from EERIE-AMIP preserve the mean climatic conditions of the source simulation as lead time increases. Comparisons with EERIE-AMIP and ERA5 provide complementary information: the former assesses consistency with the initialization dataset, while the latter indicates how closely the generated fields resemble the reanalysis used to train the AI models. 

Figure~\ref{fig:climatology-lines} shows the annual cycle of global mean temperature at 850~hPa, using forecast lead times between 10 and 20 days for the AI models. Both NeuralGCM and AIFS-CRPS reproduce the timing and amplitude of the seasonal cycle, with a minimum during boreal winter and a maximum in July. Despite being initialized from EERIE-AMIP, their global mean temperatures remain closer to ERA5 throughout most of the year. Differences relative to ERA5 are generally limited to a few tenths of a degree, whereas both models are approximately $0.2$--$0.5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ warmer than EERIE-AMIP. The agreement between the two AI models is therefore accompanied by a common departure from the mean state of their initialization dataset.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapter2//Figs/climatologie_lines.png}
    \caption{Climatological annual cycle of air temperature at 850~hPa over the period 1985--1990. The upper panel compares the daily climatologies produced by EERIE, ERA5, NeuralGCM, and AIFS-CRPS. For the AI weather models, the climatology is computed using forecast lead times between 10 and 20 days. This interval provides a common descriptive window for comparing the models and is not assumed to be free of initialization effects or forecast drift. The middle and lower panels show the temperature differences of the two AI-based models relative to ERA5 and EERIE, respectively.}
    \label{fig:climatology-lines}
\end{figure}

The lead-time-dependent biases in Figure~\ref{fig:climatology-bias} show how this departure develops. NeuralGCM exhibits a predominantly positive bias relative to EERIE-AMIP from the first five-day window onwards. The annual mean bias increases during the initial forecast stages and subsequently remains around $+0.3$--$0.4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. Relative to ERA5, the annual mean bias is substantially smaller and remains slightly positive after the first five days. Monthly differences nevertheless reveal a seasonal structure, with larger warm biases towards the end of the year. NeuralGCM therefore maintains a comparatively stable global mean over the 30-day integration, but this mean differs systematically from that of EERIE-AMIP.

AIFS-CRPS initially follows a similar pattern, with a small annual mean bias relative to ERA5 and a positive bias relative to EERIE-AMIP. After approximately 20 days, however, its behaviour changes. A cold bias develops, particularly between May and September, and intensifies in the final forecast window. By days 25--30, the annual mean bias is approximately $-0.5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ relative to ERA5 and $-0.3\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ relative to EERIE-AMIP, while several monthly biases extend beyond the lower limit of the colour scale. This confirms that the cooling identified in the individual forecasts is a recurring, seasonally dependent feature of the evaluated configuration.

The initial movement towards an ERA5-like global mean is consistent with an adjustment towards conditions represented in the training data, although the present diagnostics do not establish its mechanism. Related behaviour was reported by \citet{rackow_robustness_2024}, who found that AI weather models could retain useful forecast skill when initialized in different climate states while developing systematic temperature drift towards their training climate. Their experiments concerned colder and warmer climate regimes, whereas the present comparison concerns two representations of the same historical period. Nevertheless, both illustrate why forecast skill and preservation of the source climate must be evaluated separately.

The spatial RMSE provides a further distinction between agreement in the global mean and agreement in the geographical temperature distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:climatology-rmse}). Relative to ERA5, NeuralGCM maintains an annual mean spatial RMSE of approximately $1.1$--$1.3\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ across the forecast windows, despite its much smaller global mean bias. Relative to EERIE-AMIP, RMSE increases from approximately $0.7\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ during days 0--5 to around $1.4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ during days 25--30. Thus, the relatively stable global mean does not imply preservation of the reference spatial climatology: regional differences remain substantial and can compensate when averaged globally.

AIFS-CRPS shows a broadly comparable spatial RMSE during the first 20 days, followed by a marked deterioration. During days 25--30, the annual mean RMSE reaches approximately $2.5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ relative to ERA5 and $2.7\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ relative to EERIE-AMIP. The largest monthly errors occur during boreal summer and early autumn, matching the seasonal structure of the cold bias. These changes are much larger than the inter-member variation of the aggregated diagnostics, indicating that the deterioration is shared across the ensemble. 

The 10--20-day interval is used here as a common descriptive window for comparing the generated mean states. It spans the transition between the early forecast evolution and the strongest deterioration observed in AIFS-CRPS, although its final days may already be affected by emerging drift. This choice does not establish an optimal augmentation window, complete loss of initialization influence, or convergence to an equilibrium climate. Published experiments have shown that selected NeuralGCM configurations can reproduce climate statistics in multi-decadal integrations \citep{kochkov_neural_2024}, while AIFS-CRPS has demonstrated skill in 46-day forecasts initialized from ERA5 \citep{lang_aifs-crps_2026}. These capabilities cannot be transferred automatically to a different initialization source.

\subsection{Anomaly amplitude and spatial structure}
\label{sec:anomaly-structure}

The anomaly diagnostics examine whether the generated realizations retain anomaly amplitudes comparable to those of the source climate. This assessment complements the climatological comparison by examining daily departures from the common EERIE-AMIP reference and their evolution with forecast lead time.

Figure~\ref{fig:rms-plot} shows that both AI models maintain larger mean member RMS values than EERIE-AMIP throughout the displayed period. NeuralGCM exhibits a gradual increase from approximately 3.25 to 3.32~$^\circ$C, while EERIE-AMIP remains close to 3.18~$^\circ$C. AIFS-CRPS initially shows a smaller departure, with RMS values around 3.23--3.26~$^\circ$C, followed by a more pronounced increase around day~20, reaching approximately 3.39~$^\circ$C at day~25. The separation between individual members' RMS curves also increases at these longer leads, indicating that the amplification differs across realizations. This behaviour provides further evidence of the deterioration identified in Section~\ref{sec:performance-stability}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapter2//Figs/rms_plot.png}
    \caption{Spatial RMS of daily temperature anomalies at 850~hPa for NeuralGCM (left) and AIFS-CRPS (right), compared with EERIE-AMIP. All anomalies are calculated relative to the common EERIE-AMIP climatology for 1985--1990, using cosine-latitude weighting. Thin grey and red lines show individual AI and EERIE-AMIP members, respectively, averaged across the selected initialization dates. Thick black and red lines additionally average these RMS values across members. Both AI ensembles are initialized from EERIE-AMIP member~1.}
    \label{fig:rms-plot}
\end{figure}

These results show no general decline in anomaly amplitude relative to the EERIE-AMIP climatology. The smoother ensemble-mean fields shown earlier should therefore not be interpreted automatically as evidence that individual members lose their anomaly amplitude. \citet{lang_aifs-crps_2026} demonstrated that probabilistic training can maintain atmospheric variability that is progressively damped in deterministically trained forecasts. 

The decomposition in Figure~\ref{fig:rms-components} shows that departures from EERIE-AMIP involve both the global mean and the residual spatial structure. For NeuralGCM, the global component increases from approximately 0.41 to 0.48~$^\circ$C, compared with approximately 0.22~$^\circ$C in EERIE-AMIP. Its spatial component also increases, from approximately 3.21 to 3.31~$^\circ$C, while the reference remains around 3.18~$^\circ$C. The spatial component is much larger in absolute magnitude, demonstrating that the anomalies predominantly reflect geographical contrasts. AIFS-CRPS follows a different evolution: its global component declines after an initial maximum, whereas its spatial component increases sharply at the final available leads. The emerging spatial amplification therefore cannot be explained solely by a uniform global temperature shift. Because the global component is unsigned, its decline does not by itself establish either cooling or recovery towards the reference climate.

Figure~\ref{fig:rms-maps} identifies the regions contributing to these spatial departures. Both models exhibit larger residual anomaly amplitudes over parts of the South Pacific, South Atlantic, and southern Indian Ocean, and smaller amplitudes over parts of central and southern Africa and Australia. Several patterns persist across the displayed lead-time windows, particularly for NeuralGCM, suggesting recurrent geographical differences relative to EERIE-AMIP. AIFS-CRPS shows stronger localized departures over parts of central Asia in the 10--15-day window. Features over elevated terrain require the same caution discussed previously concerning pressure-level fields below the surface.

    \label{fig:rms-maps}

The resemblance between the two models' maps indicates common regional departures, but does not establish identical forecast dynamics. Their shared initialization source, use of ERA5 during training, and common verification climatology are plausible contributors. Moreover, both maps subtract the same EERIE-AMIP RMS field, which can contribute to their similarity. The patterns should therefore be interpreted as differences in anomaly amplitude relative to a shared reference, rather than evidence that the models reproduce the same weather trajectories.

The importance of examining spatial structure alongside aggregate scores is supported by \citet{rodwell_power_2025}. Their analysis of prototype AIFS ensembles found that broadly comparable behaviour at large scales could coexist with excessive growth of forecast variance and error at smaller scales. Those experiments involved different variables and model configurations, so they do not identify the mechanism responsible for the present results. They nevertheless demonstrate why maintaining substantial forecast activity is insufficient to establish realistic variability.

The anomaly diagnostics reveal departures from EERIE-AMIP in both global amplitude and geographical structure. NeuralGCM exhibits a gradual increase in anomaly amplitude, while AIFS-CRPS shows a sharper amplification at longer leads. These differences persist after removing the global-mean anomaly, indicating that the departures cannot be represented solely by a uniform temperature offset. Their implications for climate ensemble augmentation are considered in the following discussion.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter examined the practical and scientific requirements for augmenting climate simulations with stochastic AI weather forecasts. AIUQ provides a common workflow for preparing initial conditions, executing ensemble forecasts, and harmonizing their outputs across models. Its application to NeuralGCM and AIFS-CRPS demonstrates that substantial ensembles can be generated from EERIE-AMIP states within manageable execution times on the computing platforms used. The scientific assessment identifies a further requirement: the additional realizations must preserve the statistical properties relevant to the intended application. Computational feasibility makes the proposed augmentation possible, while the results show why its statistical suitability requires separate evaluation.

A central finding is the distinction between forecasting the evolution of the source simulation and preserving its climate. The EERIE-initialized forecasts produce aggregate CRPS values of a similar magnitude to the supplementary ERA5 experiment, yet their climatological temperature fields develop systematic departures from EERIE-AMIP. During much of the forecast period, the AI global mean temperatures are closer to ERA5 than to their initialization dataset. For an application intended to expand the sampling of EERIE-AMIP, closer agreement with ERA5 does not automatically constitute an improvement: it can also indicate that the generated samples represent a different mean state. This distinction is consistent with the coexistence of forecast skill and temperature drift reported by \citet{rackow_robustness_2024}, although their experiments examined different climate regimes. Here, shared ERA5 training provides a plausible context for the observed adjustment, but the experiment does not isolate its contribution from those of initial-state preparation and model-specific inputs.

The two models exhibit different limitations. NeuralGCM maintains comparatively stable global mean temperatures over the 30-day integrations, but retains a warm bias relative to EERIE-AMIP and increasing differences in the spatial climatology. Its behaviour therefore supports further investigation of climate ensemble augmentation without establishing that its realizations are statistically interchangeable with those of the source simulation. AIFS-CRPS version~1.0 shows a more pronounced deterioration after approximately 15--20 days, involving seasonal cooling, increasing climatological RMSE, and amplification of anomaly amplitudes. These findings limit the use of its longer forecasts in the configuration examined. The contrast between the models is empirical; it does not isolate the effects of architecture, training, or initialization procedures.

The anomaly diagnostics further show that substantial departures from the reference climatology persist within individual ensemble members. The smoothness of an ensemble-mean field therefore provides an incomplete description of the generated realizations. Both models retain anomaly amplitudes exceeding those of EERIE-AMIP, with geographically structured differences that remain after removal of the global-mean anomaly. However, these RMS measures include systematic regional offsets as well as fluctuations. Their magnitude consequently cannot establish realistic temporal variability, and the variability of member-level scores does not directly measure the diversity of the underlying weather fields. The results identify changes in anomaly amplitude and spatial structure while leaving the effective gain in weather sampling unresolved.

The choice of forecast lead time is therefore central to the proposed application. Early forecasts remain strongly conditioned by the source state, whereas longer integrations allow trajectories to diverge but also accumulate systematic departures. The present results characterize this trade-off without identifying a universally suitable forecast window. In particular, the 10--20-day interval used for the climatological comparison is a descriptive choice, and its final portion overlaps the onset of AIFS-CRPS deterioration. A useful augmentation window would need to provide additional weather diversity while maintaining acceptable agreement with the source climate, with those criteria defined for the intended variables and application.

The scope of the evaluation also limits the conclusions. The main diagnostics concern daily temperature at 850~hPa during 1985--1990, with all AI forecasts initialized from a single EERIE-AMIP member. They do not establish performance for other variables, climate states, or initialization sources. Comparisons involving high terrain are additionally sensitive to pressure-level values below the surface, whose contribution to the aggregate diagnostics has not been isolated. The reported inter-member dispersion describes variation among realizations, rather than uncertainty associated with the sampled years and initialization dates. Likewise, the different sampling of the ERA5 and EERIE forecast experiments limits conclusions about equivalent predictive skill.

Further evaluation should first quantify the size of the observed departures relative to differences among EERIE-AMIP members under matched temporal sampling. Separating systematic offsets from fluctuations would clarify whether the generated variability remains consistent with the source after accounting for mean-state differences. Examining dependence among AI realizations would then help determine how much additional sampling the ensemble provides. These checks would also inform the selection of forecast windows and the assessment of any proposed bias correction. Applications involving extremes or climate impacts would additionally require evaluation of distribution tails, event persistence, and consistency between variables.

The contribution of this chapter is thus an operational workflow and an initial assessment of statistical requirements for climate ensemble augmentation. The experiments identify model-, season-, and lead-dependent departures that must be considered before combining AI-generated realizations with the source ensemble. NeuralGCM offers a comparatively stable candidate for further evaluation, while the tested AIFS-CRPS configuration imposes a stronger limitation at longer leads. Establishing whether the resulting ensembles improve climate sampling will require demonstrating that their additional diversity is accompanied by adequate preservation of the source climate's relevant statistical properties.
