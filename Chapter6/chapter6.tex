\chapter{General Discussion}

Climate Services expose a central challenge in the application of AI: models are optimized across large datasets, but their outputs are used in particular places, under particular conditions, for particular purposes. The results of this dissertation show both the opportunities created by these technologies and the importance of this difference between the conditions under which performance is measured and those under which information becomes useful.

\section{Mode and Model Collapse}

In 2014, Goodfellow and colleagues identified a potential failure in the training of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs): the generator could map many different latent inputs to the same output, concentrating on a narrow set of samples rather than capturing the diversity of the training distribution \citep{goodfellow_generative_2014}. Known as \emph{mode collapse}, this phenomenon illustrates how a model can generate individually plausible outputs while failing to represent the range of outcomes present in the data.

With the development of Large Language Models (LLMs), researchers investigated related losses of diversity arising from different training mechanisms. In 2024, Kirk and colleagues showed that reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) reduced output diversity relative to supervised fine-tuning in the models and tasks they examined, even while improving generalization \citep{kirk_understanding_2024}. Their findings highlighted a potential tension between improving performance according to a particular evaluation criterion and preserving a broad repertoire of responses: rewarding preferred outputs can concentrate generation on a narrower set of successful patterns.

A distinct concern emerged from research on recursive training with synthetic data. In work published in 2024, Shumailov and colleagues demonstrated that successive generations of models trained on outputs from their predecessors could progressively lose information about the original data distribution, a process they termed \emph{model collapse} \citep{shumailov_ai_2024}. Sampling and approximation errors can accumulate across generations, progressively under representing rare events and distorting the distribution being learned. That same year, Gerstgrasser and colleagues showed that retaining the original data while accumulating synthetic samples avoided this degradation in their experimental settings \citep{gerstgrasser_is_2024}. 

Although these phenomena have different mechanisms, they raise a common question relevant to this dissertation: how much variability can be lost while aggregate performance remains apparently satisfactory? Describing AI as a nonlinear function optimized to minimize a loss offers a useful starting point. The outcome depends on the objective, the training distribution, and the generation procedure. For example, minimizing squared prediction error favors the conditional mean, which can smooth variability when multiple outcomes are plausible. Preference-based training can instead favor conventional responses, while recursive synthetic training can progressively lose rare cases. 

The findings of this dissertation make this concern concrete. In Chapter~4, LLMs reproduced the global magnitude of warming with considerable accuracy, while failing to capture its spatial variability. In Chapter~2, AI weather models demonstrated strong forecasting performance, yet their simulated climate rapidly drifted towards the training climatology. These results do not, by themselves, establish mode collapse or model collapse in the technical senses described above. They nevertheless expose a related limitation: skill in reproducing aggregate quantities or short-range evolution does not guarantee that a model preserves the spatial structure or longer-term behavior required for a particular application.

For Climate Services, the central question is therefore whether the information retained by a model is sufficient for the adaptation decision it is intended to support. Global averages and typical conditions provide important context, but adaptation often depends on local differences, thresholds, and extremes. Low-probability events can contribute disproportionately to climate risk, particularly when they intersect with high exposure and vulnerability. A model that accurately represents central tendencies while smoothing spatial contrasts or under representing distributional tails may consequently provide an incomplete basis for adaptation.

\section{AI as interface}

The recent application of AI tent to make it not a source of information, but as an interface to autoritative sources \citep{mialon_augmented_2023}. This approach allows to overcome the problem of the incompeteness of AI internal knowledge. We followedx this approach in different stages of this dissertation: in Chapter 2, we use the AI models starting from a numerical simulation, envisioning a use of such models as methods to expands some physics-based simulation, in Chapter 5, we used LLMs as interface to access numerical and textual evidences. 

We found that this approach is not free from risks. Even when acting on authoritative data, AI algorithms inherits some difficulties. AI weather models rapidly tend to go back to their training climatology, making difficult to envision an intensive use in climate change scenarios. In this regard, it's important to point out that this is only a preliminar examination which still require further development. Regarding LLM we found that augmentation does not automatically correspond to an increase in the scientifical perceived credibility. This finidng raises a particular concern. In fact, it's not always immediate to understand if an LLM is accessing an external source or inferring from it's internal knowledge, and the inference of fake information from the internal knowledge may sound more credible even to a climate expert audience, as shown in Chapter 5.
